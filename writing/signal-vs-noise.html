Separating Signal from Noise in Financial News

AR
Anup Roy
Applied Scientist, Inception AI · Abu Dhabi

On April 9, 2025, gold jumped 2.97% in a single day. Trump had announced sweeping tariffs. Every outlet ran it as breaking news. The next day, gold surged another 3.23% when he announced a 90-day pause. Both moves were genuinely rare — that magnitude happens less than once a month.

Three days earlier, on April 6, gold had moved 0.3%. A headline still ran: "Gold edges higher on geopolitical uncertainty." That move happens on two out of every three trading days. It was noise dressed as signal.

How different are these two cases, really? The first describes an event where less than 2% of trading days produce a move that large. The second describes something more common than a coin flip. One is worth understanding. The other exists because a journalist needed to file copy.

This distinction — whether a market move is statistically meaningful or just random fluctuation — is something you can determine with a single division, once you understand the framework behind it. This post builds that framework from scratch: what it means for a move to be "large," how to measure it, and where the method breaks down.

The ProblemA price on its own tells you nothing

Gold closed at $2,658 on January 2, 2025. Was that a good day or a bad day? Was the move large or small? Was it driven by news, or was it random drift?

You can't answer any of these questions from the price alone. A price is a point with no context. What matters is not where a price is — it's how much it moved, and whether that move was large or small relative to what normally happens.

Financial news writes headlines about prices every day. "Gold closes at $3,200." "Nifty at 24,500." These numbers feel meaningful. They aren't.

The question that actually matters

Given how this asset normally behaves, was today's move routine or unusual?

Two parts. First: what is the typical daily volatility for this asset? Second: how does today's move compare to that baseline?

Edward Thorp — mathematician, hedge fund manager, author of Beat the Dealer and A Man for All Markets — could answer this in his head in seconds. He'd hear a commentator predict the Dow would fall 9 points and know instantly: noise. We'll work through his exact reasoning later. First, the building blocks.

The Shape of NormalDaily returns — the universal language

Stop thinking in prices. Think in percentage changes.

return = (today − yesterday) ÷ yesterday × 100

A 1% move means the same thing whether gold is at $1,000 or $3,000. Percentages are comparable across assets, across time, across price levels. Raw prices aren't. For 2025 gold, the first few returns:

DayCalculationReturn
Jan 2 → Jan 3(2,645 − 2,658.90) ÷ 2,658.90−0.52%
Jan 3 → Jan 6(2,638.40 − 2,645) ÷ 2,645−0.25%
Jan 6 → Jan 7(2,656.70 − 2,638.40) ÷ 2,638.40+0.69%

You now have 251 such numbers for 2025 — one for each trading day. The question becomes: given this collection, what counts as "normal" and what counts as "unusual"?

Why the bell curve

Take those 251 returns, sort them into buckets, and count how many fall in each. The tallest bars cluster near zero. The distribution falls away symmetrically on both sides. It looks like a bell.

This isn't an assumption we impose. It's a pattern that emerges. Each daily return is the net result of dozens of independent forces — dollar moves, fund flows, central bank commentary, options expiries, geopolitical news. When many independent forces combine, the Central Limit Theorem says the result tends toward a bell shape. The bell isn't a theory we apply to gold. It's a consequence of how markets work.

Standard deviation — the step size

Every asset has its own bell. Some are wide (volatile), some are narrow (stable). One number determines the width: the standard deviation, written σ (sigma).

σ answers the question: what is the typical daily move for this asset? For 2025 gold, σ = 1.29%. For crude oil, σ = 1.98%. For S&P 500, σ = 1.18%. For the Nifty 50, σ = 0.75%.

# Computing σ from data
1. Mean of all returns      → gold 2025: +0.20%/day
2. Subtract mean from each return
3. Square each deviation
4. Average them             → variance = 1.666
5. Square root              → σ = √1.666 = 1.29%

σ is the step size on the bell curve's x-axis. One step on gold's curve = 1.29%. Two steps = 2.58%. This "step" is the natural unit for measuring how far a move is from the center.

The 68–95–99.7 rule

The bell's fixed shape gives you three numbers worth knowing: 68% of days fall within ±1σ (gold moves less than ±1.29%). 95% fall within ±2σ (only 5% exceed ±2.58%). 99.7% fall within ±3σ (beyond ±3.87% is less than 1 in 300 days). Verified against real 2025 gold data:

BandExpectedActual (251 days)
Within ±1σ68.3%74.9%
Within ±2σ95.4%94.0%
Within ±3σ99.7%99.2%

Close enough. The slight excess in ±1σ and deficit in ±3σ reflects fatter tails in real financial data — a known property. For filtering headlines, the approximation holds.

The RulerZ-score — the x-axis, relabeled

Look at the bell curve's x-axis. The tick marks at −3, −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, +3 — those are z-scores. The z-score is simply your position on the bell curve, expressed in units of σ. z = 1 means you're standing at the +1σ mark. That's all it is.

z = move ÷ σ

This is a unit conversion. The move is in %. The bell curve's x-axis is in σ-units. Division by σ converts your move into "number of steps from the center." A 1.9% move in crude oil (σ = 1.98%) is z = 0.96 — about one step, marginal. The same 1.9% move in gold (σ = 1.29%) is z = 1.47 — one and a half steps. Same absolute move, different meaning.

The mental model

A flat road. You stand at center (z = 0). The ground is marked at regular intervals. Each interval is one σ-step.

← −3σ −2σ −1σ [YOU] +1σ +2σ +3σ → −3.87% −2.58% −1.29% 0 +1.29% +2.58% +3.87% one step = σ = 1.29% (gold 2025)

A headline says gold moved +0.4%. You compute z = 0.4 ÷ 1.29 = 0.31. Less than a third of one step — you barely shuffled off the origin. A headline says gold moved +2.58%. z = 2.0. Two full steps from center — the boundary of what 95% of days produce.

Area = probability (and we want the outside)

The bell curve's total area = 100%. The question we're asking is NOT "what fraction of days land inside my position?" We want the opposite: "what fraction of days produce a move AT LEAST as large as mine?" That's the area outside — the two tails beyond your position.

Why two tails? Because direction doesn't matter for this kind of filtering. A −2% drop is as statistically notable as a +2% gain. That combined outside area is the probability we care about.

If 62% of random days produce a bigger move than yours (z = 0.5), your move is unremarkable. If only 1.2% of days produce a bigger move (z = 2.5), something genuinely unusual happened.

The Filter in PracticeHeadline to verdict

Step 1  Extract the move    "Gold surges 2.1% on Fed pivot fears" → 2.1%
Step 2  Recall σ            Gold σ = 1.29%
Step 3  Compute z           z = 2.1 ÷ 1.29 = 1.63
Step 4  Locate on table     between 1.5 (1 in 8) and 2.0 (1 in 22)
Step 5  Classify            z > 1.5 → Signal. Worth reading.

Once σ is memorized, the only real-time work is a single division. The rest is a table lookup.

Thorp's DJIA — the original insight

I heard the reporter say, "The Dow Jones Industrial Average is down 9 points to 11,075 on fears of a further interest rate rise." I mentally estimated a typical daily DJIA change at about 0.6 percent or about sixty-six points. The reported move of 9 points was less than a seventh of this… so the market action was very quiet and hardly indicative of any fearful response. Simple math allowed me to separate hype from reality. — Edward Thorp, A Man for All Markets

Walk through it: move = 9 points; σ = 0.6% × 11,000 = 66 points; z = 9 ÷ 66 = 0.14. On 89% of ordinary days the market moves more than this. Verdict: noise. The reporter attributed causation ("on fears of…") to a move so small it required no explanation at all.

The noise days tell the other half of the story: Sep 10 (+0.008%, z = 0.006), Jul 7 (−0.009%, z = 0.007), Nov 6 (−0.010%, z = 0.008). Financial news wrote stories on all three. "Gold edges higher as dollar softens." These moves are so deep inside the first σ-step that they are indistinguishable from random drift. The pattern: every z > 2.5 day had a real, identifiable macro event behind it. Every z < 0.3 day had nothing behind it except a word count requirement.

The LimitsWhere the method breaks down

The z-score filter is useful precisely because it's simple. But simplicity comes with boundaries. Three things worth being explicit about.

It tells you significance, not direction

A z > 2 move means something real drove the market. It says nothing about what happens next. May 12, 2025 was z = 2.68 — and gold fell 3.46%. Z is a filter for your information diet. It tells you which headlines to read; it does not tell you what to do once you've read them.

Sigma is not a constant — it drifts

This is the most important caveat. The entire framework rests on knowing σ, and σ is not fixed. Gold's σ ranged from 0.65% (2017, calmest year) to 1.35% (2020, COVID shock) over the decade — a 2× variation. It also shifts within a single year: in March 2025 (pre-tariff) the 60-day σ was 0.84%; by June it had nearly doubled to 1.67%. A 1.3% move was a signal in March (z = 1.55) and noise by June (z = 0.78). Crude oil is far worse — its annual σ ranged from 1.58% (2017) to 21.66% (2020, when prices went negative).

What to do about it: for casual filtering, accept the imprecision — at the extremes the framework still works under any plausible σ. For more care, recalibrate σ from the trailing 60–90 days every quarter. When in doubt, use a slightly lower σ; it makes your filter more sensitive, and it's better to read one extra headline than to miss a real event.

Fat tails — rare events are less rare than the bell predicts

Real markets have fatter tails than a perfect bell curve. Events the bell assigns near-zero probability to — z > 4, "should" happen once in 30,000 days — actually occur every few years. October 21, 2025 (z = 4.44) is one. This doesn't break the filter: z > 2.5 still means "go look." But don't use z-scores for precise probability estimation. For pricing options or sizing portfolio risk, use a distribution with fatter tails.

SummaryOne division, every morning

Memorize σ for the assets you follow. When you see a headline, divide the reported move by σ. The result — z — tells you where the move lands on the bell curve. Below 0.5: noise, skip it. Above 1.5: signal, read further. Above 2.5: something real happened, understand why.

The method has limits. Sigma drifts, so your calibration needs occasional updating. The bell curve underestimates extreme events. And statistical significance tells you nothing about direction or what to trade. But as a filter for the daily flood of financial media — for separating the 10% of headlines that carry real information from the 90% that don't — it works.

Not investment advice. This is a framework for evaluating information quality — for distinguishing meaningful market moves from routine fluctuation. It is not a trading strategy.
